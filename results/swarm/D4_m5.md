# D4 — Eigenvalue versus eigenvector stability (m5)

## 1. Conventions and corrected headline statements

All matrices are finite dimensional and self-adjoint unless stated otherwise.
Eigenvalues in Weyl's theorem are indexed in nondecreasing order.  The norm
`||·||` is the operator norm and `||·||_F` is the ordinary, unnormalised
Frobenius norm.

Let `I` be a spectral cluster of `A(t)` and `J` its complement.  At a fixed
time, put

\[
 d=\min_{\lambda\in I,\ \mu\in J}|\lambda-\mu|.
\]

Thus `d` is the **pairwise cross-gap**.  It permits the two sets to be
interleaved on the real line; this is important for the constant.  If
`d>=gamma>0` on the parameter interval, the results proved below are

\[
 \boxed{\ \|\dot P_I(t)\|\leq \frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\|\dot A(t)\|}{\gamma}\ },
 \qquad
 \boxed{\ \|\dot P_I(t)\|_F\leq\frac{\|\dot A(t)\|_F}{\gamma}\ }.
\]

The first constant is dimension-free sharp for arbitrary cross-separated
spectral sets.  If the gap is ordered, for example
`max I+gamma<=min J`, the stronger and sharp estimate

\[
 \boxed{\ \|\dot P_I(t)\|\leq\frac{\|\dot A(t)\|}{\gamma}\ }
\]

holds.  A simple eigenvalue is always covered by the constant-one analysis.

**Correction to the question.**  For a simple eigenvalue, with `P=vv*`,
`||v||=1`, and `v_perp_dot:=(1-P)v_dot`, the prompt's asserted identity
`||P_dot||_F=||v_perp_dot||` is false.  The correct identities are

\[
 \boxed{\ \|\dot P\|=\|\dot v_\perp\|,\qquad
          \|\dot P\|_F=\sqrt2\,\|\dot v_\perp\|\ }.
\]

Section 6 gives an explicit `2 x 2` counterexample to the unmodified
statement.  With the operator norm (or with Frobenius norm normalised by
`1/sqrt(2)`), the claimed identification is correct.

Cluster projectors are not magically better conditioned than every isolated
rank-one projector.  Their genuine advantage is that the **external** gap,
not the gaps **inside** the cluster or a choice of moving orthonormal basis,
controls them.  Section 7 makes this precise and gives a family for which the
cluster derivative is zero while individual eigenvector derivatives blow up.

## 2. Weyl's inequality, with a min-max proof

### Theorem 2.1

For self-adjoint `A,B` on an `n`-dimensional Hilbert space,

\[
 \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\lambda_i(A)-\lambda_i(B)|\leq\|A-B\|.
\]

### Proof

For nonzero `x`, let `R_A(x)=<x,Ax>/<x,x>`.  We first prove the
Courant--Fischer formula

\[
 \lambda_i(A)=\min_{\dim S=i}\ \max_{0\neq x\in S}R_A(x).        \tag{2.1}
\]

Take an orthonormal eigenbasis `e_1,...,e_n`, with `Ae_j=lambda_j(A)e_j`.
For `S_0=span(e_1,...,e_i)`, direct expansion gives
`max_{0!=x in S_0}R_A(x)=lambda_i(A)`, proving `<=` in (2.1).  Conversely,
if `dim S=i`, put `T=span(e_i,...,e_n)`.  Since
`dim S+dim T=i+(n-i+1)=n+1`, there is a nonzero `x` in `S intersect T`.
Expansion in the eigenbasis gives `R_A(x)>=lambda_i(A)`.  This proves `>=`.

Set `E=B-A`, `eta=||E||`, and `R_B(x)=R_A(x)+<x,Ex>/<x,x>`.  Since `E` is
self-adjoint, its numerical radius is its operator norm, so

\[
 |R_B(x)-R_A(x)|\leq\eta\qquad(x\neq0).                              \tag{2.2}
\]

For every fixed `S`, (2.2) implies
`max_{x in S}R_B(x)<=max_{x in S}R_A(x)+eta`; taking the minimum over `S`
and using (2.1) gives `lambda_i(B)<=lambda_i(A)+eta`.  Interchanging `A`
and `B` gives the opposite inequality.  Equivalently,

\[
 \lambda_i(A)-\|E\|\leq\lambda_i(A+E)\leq\lambda_i(A)+\|E\|,
\]

which is the assertion.  No simplicity or nondegeneracy is needed.  ∎

For a `C^1` self-adjoint path, Weyl also proves that each ordered eigenvalue
is continuous, since `A` itself is continuous.

## 3. The Riesz projector is `C^1`

### Proposition 3.1

Suppose the cluster and its complement are separated at `t_0`.  Then a fixed
Riesz contour defines the continuation `P_I(t)` on a neighbourhood of `t_0`,
and this continuation is `C^1`.

### Proof

Enclose the distinct points of `I` at `t_0` by finitely many pairwise
disjoint circles.  Make their radii small enough that every circle has
positive distance from all points of `I` and `J` and has winding number one
about its selected point and zero about every other eigenvalue.  Let their
union be `Gamma`.  Weyl shows that all spectra at `t` stay uniformly farther
than some `delta>0` from `Gamma` when `t-t_0` is small.  Define

\[
 P_I(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma (z-A(t))^{-1}\,dz.                 \tag{3.1}
\]

The resolvent is continuous jointly in `(z,t)` on this compact contour and
satisfies

\[
 \frac{d}{dt}(z-A(t))^{-1}
 =(z-A(t))^{-1}\dot A(t)(z-A(t))^{-1}.                                \tag{3.2}
\]

The left side is meant at fixed `z`.  Equation (3.2) follows by
differentiating `R(t)(z-A(t))=1`.  The right side of (3.2) and the
resolvent are continuous on `Gamma` times the parameter interval, so
integration and differentiation commute.  Hence `P_I` is `C^1`, and

\[
 \dot P_I=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma R(z)\dot A R(z)\,dz.               \tag{3.3}
\]

This is the Riesz spectral projector of the continued cluster; it is
orthogonal because `A(t)` is self-adjoint.  ∎

Neither analyticity nor simple eigenvalues inside `I` are used.

### The coordinate formula

At one fixed time choose an orthonormal eigenbasis with the `I` vectors
first and the `J` vectors second.  Write

\[
 A_{II}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)_{i\in I}=:E,\qquad
 A_{JJ}=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_j)_{j\in J}=:D,
\]

and `H=A_dot`.  Put `X=Q P_dot P`, the `J`-by-`I` off-diagonal block.
Differentiating `P^2=P` gives `P P_dot P=Q P_dot Q=0`, so

\[
 \dot P=\begin{pmatrix}0&X^*\\X&0\end{pmatrix}.                      \tag{3.4}
\]

Differentiating `AP=PA` and multiplying on the left by `Q` and on the right
by `P` gives the Sylvester equation

\[
 \boxed{\ DX-XE=H_{JI}\ },\qquad
 X_{ji}=\frac{H_{ji}}{\mu_j-\lambda_i}.                               \tag{3.5}
\]

In particular,

\[
 \|\dot P\|=\|X\|,\qquad \|\dot P\|_F^2=2\|X\|_F^2.                  \tag{3.6}
\]

Equation (3.6) follows by conjugating (3.4) by a suitable block-diagonal
unitary, or directly from the singular values of `X`.

## 4. Sharp projector bounds

### Theorem 4.1 (arbitrary pairwise cross-gap)

If `|mu_j-lambda_i|>=gamma>0` for every `i,j`, then

\[
 \|X\|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}\|H_{JI}\|
       \leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}\|H\|.                                \tag{4.1}
\]

Consequently `||P_dot||<=(pi/(2 gamma))||A_dot||`.  The constant `pi/2`
cannot be reduced uniformly in the dimension or the configuration of the
spectral sets.

### Theorem 4.2 (ordered gap)

If `mu_j-lambda_i>=gamma` for all `i,j` (or the reverse inequality holds),
then

\[
 \|X\|\leq\frac1\gamma\|H_{JI}\|\leq\frac1\gamma\|H\|.                 \tag{4.2}
\]

### Theorem 4.3 (Frobenius bound, sharp)

Under only the pairwise cross-gap hypothesis,

\[
 \|\dot P\|_F\leq\frac1\gamma\|H\|_F.                                 \tag{4.3}
\]

### Proofs of Theorems 4.1--4.3

The following explicit Fourier lemma is proved in Appendix A; nothing about
it is assumed here beyond that later elementary calculation.

**Fourier lemma.**  There is `k` in `L^1(R,C)` such that

\[
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|k(s)|\,ds=\frac\pi2,\qquad
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k(s)e^{isx}\,ds=\frac1x\quad(|x|\geq1).       \tag{4.4}
\]

For general `gamma>0`, define

\[
 X_0=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k(\gamma s)e^{isD}H_{JI}e^{-isE}\,ds.    \tag{4.5}
\]

The change `u=gamma s` and (4.4), applied to `x=(mu_j-lambda_i)/gamma`,
show entry by entry that `X_0=X`.  The two exponentials are unitary, so
every integrand has operator norm `||H_JI||`.  Therefore

\[
 \|X\|\leq\Big(\int |k(\gamma s)|\,ds\Big)\|H_{JI}\|
 =\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}\|H_{JI}\|,
\]

which proves (4.1).  Appendix B constructs finite-dimensional examples
whose conditioning ratios tend to `pi/2`, proving sharpness.

For an ordered gap `D-E>=gamma`, no Fourier lemma is needed.  Write

\[
 X=\int_0^\infty e^{-sD}H_{JI}e^{sE}\,ds.                             \tag{4.6}
\]

Indeed, the `(j,i)` entry is
`H_ji integral_0^infty exp(-s(mu_j-lambda_i)) ds=H_ji/(mu_j-lambda_i)`.
Moreover,

\[
 \|e^{-sD}H_{JI}e^{sE}\|\leq e^{-s\gamma}\|H_{JI}\|,
\]

so integrating gives (4.2).  Reversing the roles of `D,E` handles the
opposite ordering.

Finally, (3.5) and (3.6) give

\[
 \|\dot P\|_F^2
 =2\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}\frac{|H_{ji}|^2}{|\mu_j-\lambda_i|^2}
 \leq\frac{2}{\gamma^2}\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}|H_{ji}|^2
 \leq\frac1{\gamma^2}\|H\|_F^2,
\]

because the full Frobenius square contains the two cross-diagonal blocks
(and also the diagonal blocks).  Equality is attained, for example, by the
`2 x 2` example after Theorem 4.4 below.  ∎

The same contour used in Section 3 proves regularity, but a contour-length
bound alone need not give either sharp constant above.  The explicit
Sylvester solutions avoid dependence on contour diameter or cluster width.

### Theorem 4.4 (constant one is sharp)

Even under an ordered gap and rank one, constant one cannot be reduced.
Take

\[
 A=\operatorname{diag}(0,\gamma),\qquad
 H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad I=\{0\}.
\]

Then `||H||=1`, `X=1/gamma`, and `||P_dot||=1/gamma`.  For
`A(t)=A+tH`, the gap remains positive for small `|t|`, so this is a genuine
derivative of the path.  The same example gives equality in the Frobenius
bound (4.3).  ∎

## 5. A simple eigenvalue: exact first-order stability

Assume now that `lambda(t_0)` is simple.  Its Riesz projector has rank one.
By Section 3 it is `C^1`; the rank remains one locally because the trace of a
continuous idempotent is a continuous integer-valued function.  Also
`lambda(t)=Tr(A(t)P(t))` is `C^1`.  A smooth local unit eigenvector is
constructed without assuming one in advance: choose a fixed `xi` with `P(t_0)xi!=0`, set `u(t)=P(t)xi`, and then
`v(t)=u(t)/||u(t)||`.  On a small neighbourhood this is `C^1` and spans the
range of `P(t)`.

At the fixed time put `Q=1-P`, `w=Q v_dot`, and `H=A_dot`.  Differentiating
`v*v=1` shows that `P v_dot` is a purely imaginary phase derivative.  The
transverse part is gauge invariant in norm: replacing `v` by
`exp(i theta(t))v` multiplies `w` by `exp(i theta(t))`.

Differentiating `Av=lambda v` and `P=vv*` gives

\[
 \dot\lambda=v^*Hv,\qquad
 w=Qv_dot=\sum_{\mu\neq\lambda}
 \frac{v_\mu^*Hv}{\lambda-\mu}v_\mu,                                  \tag{5.1}
\]

and, since `w` is orthogonal to `v`,

\[
 \boxed{\ \dot P=|w\rangle\langle v|+|v\rangle\langle w|\ }.           \tag{5.2}
\]

If `w=0`, all the following norms vanish.  If `w!=0`, on the two-dimensional
space spanned by `v,w` the operator in (5.2) has eigenvalues `+||w||` and
`-||w||`; it vanishes on the orthogonal complement.  Therefore

\[
 \boxed{\ \|\dot P\|=\|w\|,\qquad \|\dot P\|_F=\sqrt2\|w\|\ }.        \tag{5.3}
\]

Let `delta=min_{mu!=lambda}|lambda-mu|`.  Equation (5.1) gives the stronger
sharp local estimates

\[
 |\dot\lambda|\leq\|H\|,\qquad
 \|w\|\leq\frac{\|QHv\|}{\delta}\leq\frac{\|H\|}{\delta}.            \tag{5.4}
\]

For a fixed `A`, the last inequality is sharp in the following exact sense:

\[
 \sup_{\|H\|=1}\|Q\dot v\|=\frac1\delta.                             \tag{5.5}
\]

To attain it, choose `mu` at distance `delta` and take
`H=|v_mu><v|+|v><v_mu|`.  This `H` is self-adjoint, has operator norm one,
and gives `w=v_mu/(lambda-mu)`.  Applying (5.3) also gives the sharp
operator- and Frobenius-norm projector sensitivities.  In particular, an
isolated rank-one projector and its transverse eigenvector have the same
worst-case conditioning **in the operator norm**, not in the unnormalised
Frobenius norm.

## 6. Explicit counterexample to the stated Frobenius identity

Let

\[
 A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\t&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \lambda_+(t)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t^2}}2,
\]
\[
 v(t)=\frac1{\sqrt{\lambda_+(t)^2+t^2}}
       \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+(t)\\t\end{pmatrix},\qquad P(t)=v(t)v(t)^*.
\]

The upper eigenvalue is simple near zero, `v` is a unit eigenvector, and
`lambda_dot_+(0)=0`.  Direct differentiation at zero gives

\[
 v(0)=\binom10,\qquad \dot v_\perp(0)=\dot v(0)=\binom01,
 \qquad
 \dot P(0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Thus

\[
 \|\dot v_\perp(0)\|=1,\qquad \|\dot P(0)\|=1,\qquad
 \|\dot P(0)\|_F=\sqrt2\neq1.
\]

This is the counterexample to the statement as written.  Equation (5.3) is
the corrected statement.

## 7. Exactly how cluster projectors are more stable

For a cluster of rank `k>1`, conjugation by any moving unitary `U(t)` inside
the cluster changes a moving orthonormal eigenbasis by `U` but leaves
`P_I` fixed.  Internal eigenvalue crossings can therefore destroy a
preferred ordering or a preferred basis without affecting the cluster
projector.  Theorems 4.1--4.3 contain **no** lower bound on internal gaps
and **no** simplicity assumption inside `I`.

The following example separates the two issues completely.  For
`epsilon>0`, define

\[
 A_\epsilon(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \epsilon&t&0\\
 t&-\epsilon&0\\
 0&0&5
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \|\dot A_\epsilon(t)\|=1.                                    \tag{7.1}
\]

For small `|t|`, the first two eigenvalues lie near zero and the third near
five.  Hence the projector onto the first-two-dimensional cluster is
literally constant:

\[
 P_I(t)=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0),\qquad \dot P_I(t)=0.
\]

By contrast, for `r(t)=sqrt(epsilon^2+t^2)`, the upper eigenvalue of the
first block is `r(t)`, with unit eigenvector

\[
 v_\epsilon(t)=
 \frac{(r(t)+\epsilon,t,0)^T}{\sqrt{(r(t)+\epsilon)^2+t^2}}.
\]

It satisfies

\[
 \dot v_\epsilon(0)=\Big(0,\frac1{2\epsilon},0\Big)^T,
 \qquad
 \|\dot P_{\lambda_+}(0)\|=\frac1{2\epsilon},                         \tag{7.2}
\]

although `||A_dot||=1` and the external gap is bounded below independently
of small `epsilon`.  The individual sensitivity diverges as the internal
gap `2 epsilon` closes; the cluster sensitivity remains zero.

At `epsilon=0`, the ordered upper eigenvalue of the two-dimensional block is `|t|`, which is not
differentiable at zero, and the projector attached to that ordered label
changes from the `(1,-1)` eigenspace to the `(1,1)` eigenspace as `t` crosses
zero.  The unordered eigenvalue branches `t,-t` do happen to have constant
eigenvectors in this special example; this is not needed for, and does not
weaken, the `epsilon>0` blow-up or the constancy of the cluster projector.

Accordingly, the precise stability claim is:

* for an isolated simple eigenvalue, use (5.3)--(5.5): no unexplained
  projector advantage exists;
* for a cluster, the gauge-invariant subspace is controlled by its external
  cross-gap alone, uniformly over internal degeneracies and internal basis
  rotations;
* this does not assert a strict inequality for every perturbation, nor a
  dimension-independent improvement for rank one.  It identifies precisely
  which instabilities the projector removes.

## Appendix A. The explicit Fourier kernel

This appendix proves (4.4) using only improper integration, Fubini/Tonelli,
and Fourier series derived below.

For `y>0`, define `w(y)=tanh(pi y/2)`.  For `t!=0`, define

\[
 r(t)=\frac{\sin t}{2}\int_0^\infty w(y)e^{-|t|y}\,dy,
 \qquad k(t)=-i r(t),
\]

and set `r(0)=k(0)=0` (the values at one point do not affect any integral).
Note that `k` is odd.

### A.1. Its exact `L^1` mass

For `y>0`, expanding in periods of `|sin t|` and integrating by parts twice
gives

\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}|\sin t|e^{-y|t|}\,dt
 =\frac{2(1+e^{-\pi y})}{(1-e^{-\pi y})(1+y^2)}.
\]

Also `w(y)=(1-e^{-pi y})/(1+e^{-pi y})`.  Tonelli's theorem for nonnegative
functions therefore yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \int_{\mathbb R}|r(t)|\,dt
 &=\int_0^\infty\frac{w(y)}2
       \left(\int_{\mathbb R}|\sin t|e^{-y|t|}\,dt\right)dy\\
 &=\int_0^\infty\frac{dy}{1+y^2}=\frac\pi2.
\end{aligned}                                                         \tag{A.1}
\]

This simultaneously proves integrability and gives the required mass.

### A.2. The odd-pole expansion and one weighted integral

The cosine coefficients of `cosh(ax)` on `[-pi,pi]` follow from

\[
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cosh(ax)\cos(nx)\,dx
 =\frac{2(-1)^n a\sinh(\pi a)}{a^2+n^2}.
\]

Evaluating its Fourier cosine series at `x=pi`, and dividing by
`sinh(pi a)`, gives

\[
 \coth z=\frac1z+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2z}{z^2+n^2\pi^2}.
\]

Using `tanh z=2 coth(2z)-coth z`, the even terms cancel, leaving

\[
 \tanh z=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{2z}{z^2+(n+\tfrac12)^2\pi^2}.
\]

At `z=pi y/2`, this becomes

\[
 yw(y)=\frac4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{y^2}{y^2+(2n+1)^2}.         \tag{A.2}
\]

For positive `alpha,beta`, partial fractions give

\[
 \int_0^\infty\frac{y^2\,dy}{(y^2+\alpha^2)(y^2+\beta^2)}
 =\frac{\pi}{2(\alpha+\beta)}.                                      \tag{A.3}
\]

The equal-parameter and zero-parameter cases follow directly or by
continuity and are included below whenever they occur.

Let `a>=0`.  All terms are nonnegative, so Tonelli permits the interchange
of the sum and integral.  Using (A.2), then (A.3) with `c=2n+1`, gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\int_0^\infty w(y)y
 \left(\frac1{y^2+a^2}-\frac1{y^2+(a+2)^2}\right)dy\\
&\quad=\frac4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac\pi2\left(\frac1{a+2n+1}-\frac1{a+2n+3}\right)
 =\frac{2}{a+1}.                                                    \tag{A.4}
\end{aligned}
\]

The series telescopes, and its finite value also justifies the preceding
Tonelli step.

### A.3. Its exterior reciprocal Fourier transform

For fixed `y>0` and real `b`,

\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{ibt}e^{-y|t|}\,dt=\frac{2y}{y^2+b^2}.
\]

Substituting `sin t=(e^{it}-e^{-it})/(2i)` yields

\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{ixt}\frac{\sin t}{2}e^{-y|t|}\,dt
 =\frac{iy}{2}\left(\frac1{y^2+(x-1)^2}-\frac1{y^2+(x+1)^2}\right).
\]

For `x>=1`, Fubini is justified by the same absolute product integral as in
(A.1).  Since `k=-ir`, it follows that

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \int_{\mathbb R}k(t)e^{ixt}\,dt
 &=\frac12\int_0^\infty w(y)y
 \left(\frac1{y^2+(x-1)^2}-\frac1{y^2+(x+1)^2}\right)dy\\
 &=\frac1{x+1-1}=\frac1x,
\end{aligned}
\]

where (A.4) was used with `a=x-1`.  For `x<=-1`, substitute `t` by `-t`,
use oddness of `k`, and apply the positive case to `-x`.  This proves the
second identity in (4.4); (A.1) proves the first.  ∎

## Appendix B. Sharpness of `pi/2`

The following explicit family shows that no dimension-free constant smaller
than `pi/2` is possible under the pairwise cross-gap hypothesis.

### B.1. Two Toeplitz symbols

For `0<|theta|<pi`, define

\[
 F(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\frac{e^{ik\theta}}{2k+1},
 \qquad
 G(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\frac{e^{ik\theta}}{(2k+1)^2}.       \tag{B.1}
\]

The needed elementary Fourier identities, for `0<u<pi`, are

\[
 \sum_{m\ \mathrm{odd}>0}\frac{\sin(mu)}m=\frac\pi4,
 \qquad
 \sum_{m\ \mathrm{odd}>0}\frac{\cos(mu)}{m^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8-\frac{\pi u}4.
\]

The first follows by letting `r` tend to one in the imaginary part of

\[
 \sum_{m\ \mathrm{odd}>0}\frac{r^m e^{imu}}m
 =\frac12\log\frac{1+re^{iu}}{1-re^{iu}}.
\]

The bounded imaginary part also permits integration of this limit over
`(0,pi)`, which gives `sum_{m odd}1/m^2=pi^2/8`.  Integrating the sine
series from `0` to `u` gives the second identity.

Pairing `k=n` with `k=-n-1` in (B.1), and putting `m=2n+1`, gives

\[
 F(\theta)=\frac{i\pi}2\operatorname{sgn}(\theta)e^{-i\theta/2},
 \qquad
 G(\theta)=e^{-i\theta/2}\left(\frac{\pi^2}4-\frac{\pi|\theta|}4\right).
\tag{B.2}
\]

Hence `|F|` has supremum `pi/2`.  The real factor in `G` decreases from
`pi^2/4` to `0` as `|theta|` goes from zero to `pi`, so `|G|` has supremum
`pi^2/4`.

Let `C` and `Y` be the bounded operators on `ell^2(Z)` obtained by
multiplication by `F` and `G` in the Fourier basis.  Their matrix entries
are precisely

\[
 C_{ji}=\frac1{2(j-i)+1},\qquad Y_{ji}=\frac1{(2(j-i)+1)^2}=C_{ji}^2,
\]

and their operator norms are `pi/2` and `pi^2/4`, respectively.

### B.2. Finite spectral-projector examples

For `N>=1`, set `J_N={-N,...,N}` and define square matrices

\[
 C_N=\Big(\frac1{2(j-i)+1}\Big)_{i,j\in J_N},\qquad
 Y_N=C_N\circ C_N,
\]

where the circle denotes entrywise multiplication.  Restriction to a
coordinate subspace gives `||C_N||<=pi/2`.  Also `||Y_N||` tends to
`pi^2/4`: approximate an almost norm-attaining `L^2` test function for `G` by
trigonometric polynomials, and note that for each fixed finitely supported
vector `x`, `(Y_Nx)_j=(Yx)_j` for `-N<=j<=N` once `N` contains the support,
so the finite-section output converges in `ell^2` to `Yx`.

Construct a diagonal `A_N` whose first spectral set is `E_N={2i:i in J_N}`
and whose second is `D_N={2j+1:j in J_N}`.  Every cross-denominator is an
odd integer, so the exact cross-gap is one.  Let `H_N` have zero diagonal
blocks and off-diagonal block `C_N` (and its adjoint), so
`||H_N||=||C_N||`.  For `X_N` in (3.5), choose the solution `Y_N`.  The
block formula (3.4) therefore gives

\[
 \frac{\|\dot P_{I,N}\|}{\|\dot A_N\|}
 =\frac{\|Y_N\|}{\|C_N\|}
 \geq\frac{2}{\pi}\|Y_N\|\longrightarrow\frac\pi2.                  \tag{B.3}
\]

The general bound gives the reverse asymptotic inequality.  For every small
`epsilon>0`, a finite self-adjoint `A_N`, perturbation `H_N`, and unit gap
one consequently have projector conditioning ratio at least
`pi/2-epsilon`.  Taking `A_N(t)=A_N+tH_N` for small `|t|` turns this into a
genuine `C^1` spectral-projector example.  This completes the sharpness
claim in Theorem 4.1.  ∎
