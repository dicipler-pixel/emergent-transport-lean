# D7. The capacity sum rule on shape space

## Conventions and the collision caveat

Let \(m_i>0\), let \(r=(r_1,\ldots,r_N)\in(\mathbb R^2)^N\), and write
\[
 M=\sum_i m_i,\qquad \langle u,v\rangle_m=\sum_i m_i u_i\cdot v_i,
 \qquad I(r)=\sum_i m_i|r_i|^2 .
\]
Assume the centre of mass is zero and \(I(r)=1\). Distances on shape space mean distances **after this normalisation**: at a general configuration the corresponding scale-invariant function is \(d_{ij}(r)/\sqrt{I(r)}\).

Initially assume \(r_i\ne r_j\) for every \(i\ne j\). This is necessary for the literal statement: \(r\mapsto |r_i-r_j|\) is not differentiable when \(r_i=r_j\). The collision issue is made explicit at the end of this note.

The quotient directions at \(r\) are spanned by
\[
 t_x=((1,0),\ldots,(1,0)),\quad t_y=((0,1),\ldots,(0,1)),\quad
 \rho r=(Jr_1,\ldots,Jr_N),\quad r,
\]
where \(J(x,y)=(-y,x)\). They represent translations, rotation, and dilation respectively. A tangent vector \(u\) is horizontal precisely when it is mass-orthogonal to these four directions. In particular, the dilation direction has unit norm because \(\langle r,r\rangle_m=I(r)=1\).

## 1. Mass-weighted inertia identity

For every configuration, with no centre-of-mass assumption,
\[
 \sum_{i<j}m_im_j|r_i-r_j|^2
 =M I(r)-\Big|\sum_i m_ir_i\Big|^2. \tag{1}
\]
Indeed, expanding the squared distances gives
\[
 \sum_{i<j}m_im_j(|r_i|^2+|r_j|^2)
 =\sum_i m_i(M-m_i)|r_i|^2
\]
and
\[
 -2\sum_{i<j}m_im_jr_i\cdot r_j
 =-\Big|\sum_i m_ir_i\Big|^2+\sum_i m_i^2|r_i|^2.
\]
Adding these two identities proves (1). At zero centre of mass and unit inertia it reduces to
\[
 \boxed{\ \sum_{i<j}m_im_jd_{ij}^2=M\ }. \tag{2}
\]

## 2. One distance gradient and its horizontal part

Put
\[
 e_{ij}=\frac{r_i-r_j}{d_{ij}},\qquad d_{ij}=|r_i-r_j|>0.
\]
Since \(\partial d_{ij}/\partial r_i=e_{ij}\) and \(\partial d_{ij}/\partial r_j=-e_{ij}\), the gradient with respect to \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_m\) is the vector \(G^{ij}\) whose only nonzero particle components are
\[
 G_i^{ij}=\frac1{m_i}e_{ij},\qquad
 G_j^{ij}=-\frac1{m_j}e_{ij}. \tag{3}
\]
It satisfies \(\langle G^{ij},u\rangle_m=dd_{ij}(r)[u]\) for every \(u\). Consequently,
\[
 \|G^{ij}\|_m^2=\frac1{m_i}+\frac1{m_j}. \tag{4}
\]
Translation and rotation invariance follow directly from (3):
\[
 \sum_km_kG_k^{ij}=0,\qquad
 \langle G^{ij},\rho r\rangle_m
 =e_{ij}\cdot J(r_i-r_j)=d_{ij}\,e_{ij}\cdot Je_{ij}=0. \tag{5}
\]
Euler's identity for this positively \(1\)-homogeneous function, or a direct calculation using (3), gives
\[
 \langle G^{ij},r\rangle_m=e_{ij}\cdot(r_i-r_j)=d_{ij}. \tag{6}
\]
Define
\[
 H^{ij}=G^{ij}-d_{ij}r. \tag{7}
\]
By (5), (6), zero centre of mass, and \(I=1\), \(H^{ij}\) is orthogonal to translations, rotation, and dilation. It is therefore horizontal. Moreover, on any horizontal variation the unit-inertia constraint is stationary, so the derivative of the normalised shape distance is exactly \(dd_{ij}\). Hence \(H^{ij}\) is the shape gradient (its horizontal lift), and (4), (6), and \(\|r\|_m=1\) yield
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \|\nabla_{\rm shape}d_{ij}\|_m^2
 &=\|G^{ij}\|_m^2-2d_{ij}\langle G^{ij},r\rangle_m+d_{ij}^2\|r\|_m^2\\
 &=\frac1{m_i}+\frac1{m_j}-d_{ij}^2. \end{aligned} \tag{8}
\]
The submersion definition of the quotient metric says that the horizontal lift preserves norm, so no additional factor occurs.

Equation (7) also identifies the scale contribution exactly: the component along the **unit** radial vector \(r\) is \(d_{ij}\), and its squared norm is \(d_{ij}^2\). Equivalently, along \(r(s)=e^sr\) one has \(d_{ij}(r(s))=e^sd_{ij}(r)\), whose derivative at \(s=0\) is \(d_{ij}(r)\). Thus the scale direction carries \(\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}^2\), not a multiple of it.

## 3. Sum over pairs and equal masses

Every index \(i\) occurs in exactly \(N-1\) unordered pairs. Summing (8) therefore proves the requested identity:
\[
 \boxed{\ \sum_{i<j}\|\nabla_{\rm shape}d_{ij}\|_m^2
 =(N-1)\sum_i\frac1{m_i}-\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}^2.\ } \tag{9}
\]
For \(N\ge2\) equal unit masses (\(m_i=1\), so \(M=N\)), identity (2) gives \(\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}^2=N\). Substitution into (9) gives
\[
 \boxed{\ \sum_{i<j}\|\nabla_{\rm shape}d_{ij}\|_m^2
 =N(N-1)-N=N(N-2).\ } \tag{10}
\]
For \(N=2\), both sides vanish, consistently with the sole normalised distance being constant on the zero-dimensional shape space.

## 4. Literal statement at collisions: counterexample and correction

If any two bodies coincide, the left side of (9) as written contains an undefined gradient. This is not repaired by the quotient normalisation. For example, take three unit masses and the zero-centre-of-mass path
\[
 r_1(\epsilon)=(1,\epsilon),\quad r_2(\epsilon)=(1,-\epsilon),\quad r_3(\epsilon)=(-2,0).
\]
Its inertia is \(6+2\epsilon^2\), so after unit-inertia normalisation
\[
 d_{12}(\epsilon)=\frac{2|\epsilon|}{\sqrt{6+2\epsilon^2}}.
\]
This is translation- and rotation-invariant and has no derivative at the collision \(\epsilon=0\).

The corrected statement is: **(9) holds on the unit-inertia, zero-centre-of-mass configuration space minus the collision locus, with distances understood after unit-inertia normalisation.** Away from collisions all gradients above are ordinary smooth gradients. At a collision one may discuss directional derivatives such as the absolute value above, but not the Riemannian gradient appearing in (9).
