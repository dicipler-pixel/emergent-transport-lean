# D5. Cluster protection through an exceptional point

## Statement to be proved, and a necessary correction

All matrix norms below are induced by the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb C^d$. We use the analytic family
$$
B(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\t&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It is polynomial, hence analytic, in both real and complex $t$. It has an exceptional point (EP) at $t=0$: the two eigenvalues and their one-dimensional eigenspaces coalesce, while $B(0)$ is not diagonalizable.

The asserted eigenvector blow-up in part (i) of D5 is **false as written**. The following exact facts about this example disprove it as an unqualified general assertion:

* unit right eigenvectors have norm $1$ and coalesce, with separation asymptotic to $2|t|^{1/2}$;
* suitably normalized dual (left) eigenvectors, the individual spectral projectors, and the eigenvector-basis condition number diverge like $|t|^{-1/2}$, not like $|t|^{1/2}$.

The precise rates and constants are proved below. No genericity assumption is used.

## 1. Puiseux expansion and failure of eigenvalue differentiability

The characteristic polynomial is
$$
\det(\lambda I-B(t))=\lambda^2-t.                 \tag{1}
$$
On any slit disk about $t=0$, choose an analytic branch $s(t)^2=t$. The two eigenvalues and corresponding right eigenvectors are
$$
\lambda_\pm=\pm s,\qquad v_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}1\\\pm s\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s^2=t.                                      \tag{2}
$$
Indeed, $B(t)v_\pm=(\pm s,t)^T=\pm s(1,\pm s)^T$. Analytic continuation once around $t=0$ changes $s$ to $-s$ and interchanges the branches. Thus the Puiseux series are exactly $\lambda_+(t)=t^{1/2}$ and $\lambda_-(t)=-t^{1/2}$: both exponents are $1/2$, and there is one ramification cycle of length $2$.

At $t=0$,
$$
B(0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B(0)^2=0,
$$
but $B(0)\ne0$. Hence $\ker B(0)=\operatorname{span}\{e_1\}$ is one-dimensional. So $0$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ but geometric multiplicity $1$, confirming an EP rather than a merely degenerate, diagonalizable eigenvalue.

The non-differentiability claim does not depend on a labelling of the branches. If $\mu(t)$ is either eigenvalue, for real $t$ in a two-sided neighbourhood of zero, then (1) gives $\mu(t)^2=t$ and $\mu(0)=0$, hence
$$|\mu(t)|=|t|^{1/2}.                                      \tag{3}
$$
If $\mu$ were differentiable at zero, the definition of the derivative would imply $\mu(t)=O(|t|)$, contradicting (3) for sufficiently small nonzero $t$. In particular, no relabelling makes the eigenvalues differentiable at the EP.

## 2. Exact eigenvector, dual-vector, and eigenprojector asymptotics

Put $r=|t|$ for $t\ne0$, and normalize the right eigenvectors by
$$u_\pm=\frac{v_\pm}{\sqrt{1+r}}.                       \tag{4}
$$
Both are unit vectors. Their separation and overlap satisfy
$$
\|u_+-u_-\|_2=\frac{2\sqrt r}{\sqrt{1+r}},
\qquad u_+^*u_-=\frac{1-r}{1+r}.                        \tag{5}
$$
These follow directly from $u_+-u_-=(0,2s)^T/\sqrt{1+r}$ and $\overline{s}s=r$. Thus both unit eigenvectors converge to $e_1$, and their distance is $2|t|^{1/2}(1+O(|t|))$. This is the correct $+|t|^{1/2}$ coalescence statement.

To quantify the blow-up, form the eigenvector matrix $V=(v_+\ v_-)$. Then
$$
V=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\s&-s\end{pmatrix},\qquad
V^*V=\begin{pmatrix}1+r&1-r\\1-r&1+r\end{pmatrix}.       \tag{6}
$$
The eigenvalues of $V^*V$ are $2$ and $2r$, with respective eigenvectors $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$. Therefore the two singular values of $V$ are $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt{2r}$, and its spectral condition number is exactly
$$
\kappa_2(V)=\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{2r}}=r^{-1/2}=|t|^{-1/2}. \tag{7}
$$
Multiplying both columns by the common normalization $(1+r)^{-1/2}$ does not change $\kappa_2$.

The rows of $V^{-1}$ give dual left eigenvectors
$$
w_\pm=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\pm s^{-1}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad w_jv_k=\delta_{jk},\qquad w_\pm B=\pm s\,w_\pm.   \tag{8}
$$
Their norms are
$$\|w_\pm\|_2=\frac12\sqrt{1+r^{-1}}\sim\frac12|t|^{-1/2}. \tag{9}
$$
The associated rank-one spectral projectors are $P_\pm=v_\pm w_\pm$, so
$$
P_\pm=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\pm s^{-1}\\\pm s&1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac12\left(I\pm\frac{B(t)}s\right).                  \tag{10}
$$
They satisfy $B=\lambda_+P_++\lambda_-P_-$ and $P_++P_-=I$ for $t\ne0$. Since $P_\pm$ has rank one, its operator norm is the product of the Euclidean norms of its right vector and its left row vector:
$$
\|P_\pm\|=\|v_\pm\|_2\|w_\pm\|_2
=\frac{1+r}{2\sqrt r}
=\frac12|t|^{-1/2}+O(|t|^{1/2}).                       \tag{11}
$$
The Frobenius norm has the same value because $P_\pm$ has rank one. Equations (7), (9), and (11) are the correct blow-up statements. There can be no continuous invertible diagonalizing eigenvector basis through $t=0$: such a basis would make $B(0)$ similar to a diagonal matrix, contradicting that the nonzero matrix $B(0)$ is nilpotent.

## 3. The Riesz projector for the colliding pair is identically the identity

Let $\Gamma=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$, traversed counterclockwise. For $|t|<1$, the two poles $\pm s$ lie inside $\Gamma$. Since $(zI-B)(zI+B)=(z^2-t)I$, the resolvent is
$$
R_B(z,t):=(zI-B(t))^{-1}=\frac{zI+B(t)}{z^2-t}.          \tag{12}
$$
It is jointly analytic in $(z,t)$ for $z\in\Gamma$ and $|t|<1$, despite the EP at $t=0$.

The Riesz projector containing both eigenvalues is, for $t\ne0$,
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma R_B(z,t)\,dz
=\operatorname*{Res}_{z=s}R_B+\operatorname*{Res}_{z=-s}R_B. \tag{13}
$$
The two residues are computed from (12):
$$
\operatorname*{Res}_{z=s}R_B=\frac{sI+B}{2s}
=\frac12\left(I+\frac{B}{s}\right),
$$
$$
\operatorname*{Res}_{z=-s}R_B=\frac{-sI+B}{-2s}
=\frac12\left(I-\frac{B}{s}\right).
$$
Their sum is $I$. At $t=0$ the same conclusion follows directly from
$$R_B(z,0)=z^{-1}I+z^{-2}B(0),$$
because $\int_\Gamma z^{-1}dz=2\pi i$ and $\int_\Gamma z^{-2}dz=0$. Consequently, for every $|t|<1$, including $t=0$,
$$
P_{\rm pair}(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma(zI-B(t))^{-1}dz=I. \tag{14}
$$
It is therefore analytic through zero and, more strongly, constant. The residues in (10) individually contain $s^{-1}$, but their singular off-diagonal terms cancel exactly in $P_++P_-=I$. The total Riesz projection only needs the resolvent on the fixed contour, which stays a positive distance from the coalescing spectrum.

## 4. A separated block-diagonal $4\times4$ extension

We give a concrete analytic family satisfying (iii):
$$
A_4(t)=B(t)\oplus C(t),\qquad
C(t)=\begin{pmatrix}3+t&0\\0&4-t\end{pmatrix}.         \tag{15}
$$
For complex $|t|\le1/4$, the EP block's spectrum is contained in the disk $\{|z|\le1/2\}$, while
$$|3+t|\ge\frac{11}{4},\qquad |4-t|\ge\frac{15}{4}.$$
Hence the distance between the two spectral groups is at least
$$\min\left\{\frac{11}{4},\frac{15}{4}\right\}-\frac12=\frac94=:\gamma.$$
On the strict domain $|t|<1/4$, the fixed contour $\Gamma=\{|z|=1\}$ encloses precisely the two eigenvalues of $B(t)$ and excludes those of $C(t)$.

The resolvent is block diagonal:
$$
R_4(z,t)=R_B(z,t)\oplus
\begin{pmatrix}(z-3-t)^{-1}&0\\0&(z-4+t)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{16}
$$
For every $z\in\Gamma$ and $|t|<1/4$, its denominators are nonzero: $|z^2-t|\ge1-|t|>0$, $|z-3-t|\ge|3+t|-1>0$, and $|z-4+t|\ge|4-t|-1>0$. Thus $R_4(z,t)$ is analytic in $t$ on the integration contour. Its contour integral is the Riesz projector
$$
P_4(t)=P_{\rm pair}(t)\oplus0_2
=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0,0).                            \tag{17}
$$
Here the $C$-resolvents integrate to zero because their poles lie outside $\Gamma$, and (14) evaluates the $B$-block. Therefore
$$
P_4(t)\text{ is analytic through }0,
\qquad \dot P_4(t)=0,
\qquad \boxed{\|\dot P_4(t)\|=0}.                       \tag{18}
$$
This is the sharpest possible derivative bound, and is stronger than any positive bound involving $\gamma$ or $\|\dot A_4\|$. The direct-sum structure alone makes the coordinate invariant subspace constant; the gap ensures that it is also the spectrally separated Riesz cluster selected by a fixed contour.

### If a coupled, non-block-diagonal extension was intended

The zero bound is special to the literal block-diagonal hypothesis. For completeness, suppose $A(t)$ is any analytic matrix family and a fixed finite contour $\Gamma$ excludes $\sigma(A(t))$ for $t$ in a compact parameter set. The Riesz projector is analytic: difference quotients of $R(z,t)$ converge uniformly on $\Gamma$, allowing differentiation under the integral. From $R(z,t)(zI-A(t))=I$ we obtain
$$
\frac{d}{dt}R(z,t)=R(z,t)\dot A(t)R(z,t),
\qquad
\dot P(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma R\dot A R\,dz.        \tag{19}
$$
If $L$ is the length of $\Gamma$ and $M=\sup_{z\in\Gamma,t}\|R(z,t)\|$, the triangle inequality for integrals gives the local non-asymptotic bound
$$
\boxed{\quad\|\dot P(t)\|\le\frac{L M^2}{2\pi}\,\|\dot A(t)\|.\quad} \tag{20}
$$
For a normal matrix, unitary diagonalization gives $\|R(z,t)\|=\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(A(t)))^{-1}$; if $\Gamma$ lies in a gap of width $\gamma$ with distance at least $\gamma/2$ from both spectral groups, (20) becomes $2L\|\dot A(t)\|/(\pi\gamma^2)$. A normal matrix cannot itself have an EP, so for an EP family one must use a resolvent bound such as $M$, not assume normality. In general non-normal case, an eigenvalue gap alone does not control $\|R\|$: for $E_N=\begin{pmatrix}0&N\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, the spectrum is $\{0,1\}$ for every $N$, but the $(1,2)$ entry of $(zI-E_N)^{-1}$ at $z=1/2$ has modulus $4N$.

## 5. Why the EP is invisible to the cluster projector

There are two different gaps. The **external gap** is the distance from the colliding pair to the remaining spectrum. It permits one fixed contour and a uniformly analytic resolvent on that contour. The **internal gap** is $|\lambda_+-\lambda_-|=2|s|$, which tends to zero. Isolating either internal eigenvalue divides by this internal gap; explicitly, (10) has terms proportional to $s^{-1}$. Including both eigenvalues sums the two residues and cancels those terms. At $t=0$ there is no two-dimensional decomposition into distinct one-dimensional eigenspaces, but there is still the two-dimensional generalized eigenspace, with total spectral projector $I$. This is why the cluster/block projector is protected while individual eigenvectors and individual spectral projectors are not.

## 6. Deterministic numerical verification

The accompanying script `check_D5_m4.py` uses only NumPy. It checks all exact algebraic identities, the Puiseux/eigenvector/condition-number scaling formulas, the individual-projector divergence formula, and the $2\times2$ and $4\times4$ contour projectors. Contour integrals are evaluated by a 256-point trapezoidal rule (exponentially accurate for these periodic analytic integrands). It also verifies the uniform gap $\gamma=9/4$ and evaluates the contour-integral expression for $\dot P_4$, reporting its numerical residual from the proved value zero.
