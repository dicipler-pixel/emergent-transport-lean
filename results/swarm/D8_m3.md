# D8 — Exact distance-map spectrum on the three-body shape sphere

## Statement and qualifications

Use the sign convention
\[
\begin{aligned}
x&:=d_{12}^2=2\cos^2\alpha,\\
y&:=d_{13}^2=\tfrac12+\sin^2\alpha+\sqrt3\,\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cos\psi,\\
z&:=d_{23}^2=\tfrac12+\sin^2\alpha-\sqrt3\,\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cos\psi .
\end{aligned}
\]
The other sign in the question merely interchanges \(y,z\), and none of the conclusions changes. Put
\[
s=\sin\alpha,\quad c=\cos\alpha,\quad C=sc,\quad p=\cos\psi,\quad q=\sin\psi,\quad u=s^2.
\]
The quotient metric is \(d\alpha^2+C^2d\psi^2\). The target of
\(\Phi=(\sqrt x,\sqrt y,\sqrt z)\) has its Euclidean metric.

The **regular domain** is the open part of the shape sphere where
\(0<\alpha<\pi/2\) and \(x,y,z>0\). On it \(\Phi\) is smooth. We prove there that
\[
\operatorname{tr}(M^*M)=3,\qquad
\det(M^*M)=\frac{9C^2q^2}{xyz}
=\frac{3\bigl(2(xy+yz+zx)-x^2-y^2-z^2\bigr)}{4xyz}.   \tag{1}
\]
Consequently the squared singular values, ordered as
\(\sigma_1^2\ge\sigma_2^2\), satisfy
\[
\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2=3,\qquad \sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2=\det(M^*M),\qquad
\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2=\sqrt{9-4\det(M^*M)}.             \tag{2}
\]
At every non-collision collinear shape the rank is \(1\) and the gap is \(3\); at every other regular shape the rank is \(2\). The determinant is at most \(9/4\), with equality precisely at
\((\alpha,\psi)=(\pi/4,\pi/2)\) and \((\pi/4,3\pi/2)\), the two oriented equilateral shapes.

There are three binary-collision points. The distance map is not differentiable there, so \(M\), its rank, and its singular values are not defined there. Moreover, the determinant in (1) has no continuous extension to a collision: at the \(23\)-collision it has limit \(0\) along the collinear meridian and limit \(2\) along \(\alpha=\pi/6\). The rank and collinearity assertions below are therefore assertions on the non-collision locus, not at its collision endpoints.

Also, the literal claim that **\(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2\) itself closes quadratically** at an equilateral point is false. The correct local statement is
\[
(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2)^2=9\rho^2+O(\rho^3),\qquad
\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2=3\rho+O(\rho^2),                  \tag{3}
\]
where \(\rho\) is geodesic distance from that equilateral point. Thus it is the *square* of the gap that closes quadratically; the gap closes linearly.

## 1. Horizontal gradients and the trace

A preshape is \(r=(r_1,r_2,r_3)\in(\mathbb R^2)^3\) with
\[
r_1+r_2+r_3=0,\qquad I(r)=|r_1|^2+|r_2|^2+|r_3|^2=1.    \tag{4}
\]
The quotient uses the restriction of the Euclidean metric to this unit preshape sphere and its \(U(1)\)-action \((r_1,r_2,r_3)\mapsto(e^{i\theta}r_1,e^{i\theta}r_2,e^{i\theta}r_3)\). Let \(Jr=ir\). A vector \(v\) is horizontal precisely when it is tangent to (4) and \(\langle v,Jr\rangle=0\).

For one distance \(d_a=d_{ij}\), let \(u_a\) be its Euclidean gradient in configuration space: its \(i\)-th and \(j\)-th components are \((r_i-r_j)/d_{ij}\) and \(-(r_i-r_j)/d_{ij}\), and its third component is zero. Translation invariance gives \(\sum_k(u_a)_k=0\). Direct evaluation gives
\[
\|u_a\|^2=2,\qquad \langle u_a,r\rangle=d_a,\qquad \langle u_a,Jr\rangle=0. \tag{5}
\]
The last two identities are respectively Euler scaling and rotation invariance. Define
\[
w_a=u_a-d_a r.                                            \tag{6}
\]
By (4)–(5), \(w_a\) is tangent to the preshape sphere and is horizontal. Moreover \(dd_a(v)=\langle w_a,v\rangle\) for every horizontal \(v\), so \(w_a\) is the horizontal lift of the shape gradient. Its squared norm is
\[
\|\nabla^{\rm shape}d_a\|^2=\|w_a\|^2
=2-2d_a^2+d_a^2=2-d_a^2.                                 \tag{7}
\]
In any orthonormal tangent frame, the diagonal entries of \(M^*M\) are these three squared gradient norms. Summing (7), and using \(x+y+z=3\), gives
\[
\operatorname{tr}(M^*M)=\sum_a(2-d_a^2)=6-(x+y+z)=3.       \tag{8}
\]
This proves part (i) without differentiating through square roots.

## 2. Exact determinant

On the regular domain use the orthonormal coframe \((d\alpha,C\,d\psi)\). Write \(A_a=\partial_\alpha d_a^2\) and \(B_a=\partial_\psi d_a^2\), where \(a\) runs over \(x,y,z\). Then
\[
M_{a1}=\frac{A_a}{2\sqrt a},\qquad
M_{a2}=\frac{B_a}{2C\sqrt a}.                              \tag{9}
\]
Lagrange's identity for a sum of three outer products in two dimensions yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\det(M^*M)
&=\frac1{16}\left[\Bigl(\sum_a\frac{A_a^2}{a}\Bigr)
 \Bigl(\sum_a\frac{B_a^2}{C^2a}\Bigr)
 -\Bigl(\sum_a\frac{A_aB_a}{Ca}\Bigr)^2\right]\\
&=\frac1{16C^2}\sum_{a<b}\frac{(A_aB_b-A_bA_a)^2}{ab}. 
\end{aligned}                                                  \tag{10}
\]
This is just Cauchy–Binet applied to the three rows of \(M\).

Differentiating the definitions gives
\[
A_x=-4C,\quad A_y=2C+\sqrt3(c^2-s^2)p,\quad
A_z=2C-\sqrt3(c^2-s^2)p,
\]
\[
B_x=0,\qquad B_y=-\sqrt3Cq,\qquad B_z=\sqrt3Cq,
\]
so in particular \(A_y+A_z=4C\). All three wedge products in (10) therefore have the same square:
\[
(A_xB_y)^2=(A_xB_z)^2=(A_yB_z-A_zB_y)^2=48C^4q^2.
\]
Substitution into (10) proves the first equality in (1):
\[
\det(M^*M)=\frac{3C^2q^2(x+y+z)}{xyz}=\frac{9C^2q^2}{xyz}.  \tag{11}
\]

For the rational form, define the Heron expression
\[
\Delta:=2(xy+yz+zx)-x^2-y^2-z^2.                           \tag{12}
\]
Since \(x+y+z=3\), we have \(\Delta=4(xy+yz+zx)-9\). Also
\[
Y:=\tfrac12+u=\tfrac{y+z}{2},\qquad C^2=u(1-u),\qquad x=2(1-u),\qquad yz=Y^2-3C^2p^2.
\]
Elementary expansion gives
\[
x(3-x)+Y^2=2(1-u)(1+2u)+(u+\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac94+3C^2.
\]
Hence
\[
xy+xz+yz=x(3-x)+yz=\tfrac94+3C^2(1-p^2)
\]
and
\[
\Delta=12C^2q^2.                                            \tag{13}
\]
Combining (11) and (13) proves the second equality in (1). Equation (2) now follows from the characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2-3\lambda+\det(M^*M)\).

## 3. Collinearity, rank, and the largest gap

With \(a=\sqrt x,b=\sqrt y,c=\sqrt z\), Heron's factorization is
\[
\Delta=(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c).                       \tag{14}
\]
At a non-collision configuration \(a+b+c>0\), and at most one of the three remaining factors can be negative: the sum of any two is twice one of \(a,b,c\). Since \(\Delta=12C^2q^2\ge0\), (14) shows that \(\Delta>0\) exactly when all strict triangle inequalities hold, and \(\Delta=0\) exactly when one triangle inequality is equality. The latter is precisely the collinear locus.

The positive denominator \(xyz\) in (1) now gives
\[
\det(M^*M)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Delta=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{collinear}.              \tag{15}
\]
Away from collinearity the product of the two eigenvalues is positive and their sum is \(3\), so both are positive and the rank is \(2\). On the regular collinear locus their product is zero and sum is \(3\), hence they are \(3,0\), the rank is \(1\), and the gap is \(3\). Finally,
\[
\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2=3-2\sigma_2^2\le3,
\]
with equality exactly when \(\sigma_2^2=0\). This proves the maximal-gap assertion. The pole \(\alpha=0\), although not in this coordinate chart, is the non-collision collinear shape \((a,b,c)=(\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)\); the same conclusion holds there by a coordinate-free continuity argument and smoothness of \(\Phi\) at that ordinary triangle.

## 4. Global maximum of the determinant

First isolate the collision product:
\[
yz=Y^2-3C^2p^2
=4(u-\tfrac14)^2+3C^2(1-p^2)
=4(u-\tfrac14)^2+3C^2q^2.                                  \tag{16}
\]
Using (16), direct expansion gives the sum-of-squares identity
\[
xyz-4C^2q^2
=\frac{1-u}{2}\left[(1-p^2)(1-2u)^2+p^2(1-4u)^2\right]\ge0. \tag{17}
\]
Indeed, the left side is \(2(1-u)yz-4C^2(1-p^2)\); substituting (16) and multiplying out gives exactly the right side. On the regular domain \(xyz>0\), and (11) and (17) imply
\[
\det(M^*M)=\frac94\frac{4C^2q^2}{xyz}\le\frac94.            \tag{18}
\]

If equality holds at a regular shape, (17) must vanish. If \(p=0\), the first square forces \(u=1/2\). If \(0<p^2<1\), the two squares would have to vanish simultaneously, requiring the incompatible conditions \(u=1/2\) and \(u=1/4\). If \(p^2=1\), the second square forces \(u=1/4\), but then (16) gives \(yz=0\), a collision excluded from the regular domain. Thus regular equality occurs only for \(u=1/2,p=0\), namely \(\alpha=\pi/4\) and \(\psi=\pi/2\) or \(3\pi/2\). At those points \(x=y=z=1\) and (1) gives determinant \(9/4\).

## 5. The precise equilateral closing rate

Near \((\alpha,\psi)=(\pi/4,\pi/2)\), set
\[
X=\alpha-\pi/4,\qquad Y=(\psi-\pi/2)/2,\qquad \rho^2=X^2+Y^2. \tag{19}
\]
Substitution of \(\alpha=\pi/4+X\) and \(\psi=\pi/2+2Y\) into the smooth closed form (1), followed by Taylor expansion, gives
\[
C^2q^2=\tfrac14-(X^2+Y^2)+O(\rho^3),\qquad
xyz=1-3(X^2+Y^2)+O(\rho^3),                               \tag{20}
\]
and hence
\[
\det(M^*M)=\tfrac94-\tfrac94\rho^2+O(\rho^3),
\qquad 9-4\det(M^*M)=9\rho^2+O(\rho^3).                     \tag{21}
\]
For completeness, the two calculations in (20) follow from
\(x=1-2X+O(X^2)\), \(y=1-\sqrt3Y+O(\rho^2)\), \(z=1+\sqrt3Y+O(\rho^2)\), together with \(C^2=1/4-X^2+O(X^4)\) and \(q^2=1-4Y^2+O(Y^4)\). Multiplying these expansions gives the displayed quadratic terms; all functions are analytic near the equilateral point.

Also \(d\alpha^2+C^2d\psi^2=(1+O(\rho^2))(dX^2+dY^2)\), so the coordinate radius \(\rho\) agrees with geodesic distance to the required order. Equation (2) and the square-root expansion \(\sqrt{1+h}=1+O(h)\) now prove (3). Symmetry \(\psi\mapsto2\pi-\psi\), which leaves (1) invariant, gives the same expansion at \((\pi/4,3\pi/2)\).

In particular, along \(X=t,Y=0\),
\[
\sigma_1^2-\sigma_2^2=3|t|+O(t^2),
\]
not \(O(t^2)\). This explicitly disproves the literal quadratic-closing claim for the unsquared gap.

## 6. Collision points and non-differentiability

Equation (16) shows \(yz=0\) exactly when \(u=1/4,p^2=1\), giving the \(13\)- and \(23\)-collisions; \(x=0\) gives the \(12\)-collision at \(\alpha=\pi/2\). There are no triple collisions on \(I=1\).

For example, at the \(23\)-collision \((\alpha,\psi)=(\pi/6,0)\),
\[
z=\tfrac12+s^2-\sqrt3sc=\tfrac12(c-\sqrt3s)^2,
\qquad d_{23}=\frac{|c-\sqrt3s|}{\sqrt2}.                    \tag{22}
\]
Along \(\psi=0\), its left and right derivatives with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\pi/6\) are respectively \(-\sqrt2\) and \(+\sqrt2\), so \(d_{23}\), and hence \(\Phi\), is not differentiable. The same formula applies to \(d_{13}\) at \((\pi/6,\pi)\). At \((\alpha,\psi)=(\pi/2,\cdot)\), \(d_{12}=\sqrt2c\) has the analogous absolute-value cusp in a smooth local coordinate through the pole. Thus the three collisions must be excluded from every statement about \(M\) or its rank.

There is not even a continuous determinant extension. Along the collinear path \(\psi=0\), \(\alpha\to\pi/6\), every nonsingular determinant is zero. On the other hand, at fixed \(\alpha=\pi/6\) we have \(C^2=3/16\), \(x=3/2\), \(y=3(1+p)/4\), and \(z=3(1-p)/4\). For \(q\ne0\), (11) consequently gives
\[
\det(M^*M)=\frac{9(3/16)q^2}{(3/2)(9/16)q^2}=2.
\]
Hence the limit as \(\psi\to0\) on this second path is \(2\), not \(0\). Heron's numerator \(\Delta\) does extend continuously by zero, but the normalized determinant does not.
